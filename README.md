# PES news feed

A live energy and commodities news feed for the PES trading terminal. Pulls
operational disclosures, trade press and broad news, scores everything for
relevance to a UK gas and power book, deduplicates it and serves a single JSON
file that the terminal widget polls.

**Running cost: zero.** No paid APIs, no model calls, no metered hosting.

---

## How it fits together

```
  sources                ingest (cron, 5 min)              widget
  ─────────────────      ────────────────────────          ──────────────
  REMIT UMM feeds  ──┐   fetch                             polls
  ENTSO-E outages  ──┤   normalise                         data/news.json
  Elexon REMIT     ──┼──▶ score      ──▶ data/news.json ──▶ once a minute
  GIE AGSI+        ──┤   deduplicate                       renders a drawer
  publisher RSS    ──┤   rate-limit alerts                 flashes red on a
  GDELT            ──┘   retain 14 days                    critical
```

The JSON file *is* the API. There is no server, no database and no endpoint to
maintain. Serve it as a static asset next to the terminal.

## Setup

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt

python src/ingest.py --check     # test every source, see what works
python src/ingest.py --demo      # build from fixtures, no network needed
python src/ingest.py             # the real thing
python tests/test_pipeline.py    # regression suite, 20 checks
python build_demo.py             # standalone widget demo with data baked in
```

Start with `--check`. It tests every configured source and prints a table of
what is live, what is empty and what is broken. Fix or disable anything that
fails, then run it for real.

## The two files you will actually edit

### `config/themes.json`

What counts as relevant, and how much. This is the file that keeps the system
current without a developer.

It deliberately contains **no named conflicts, no named companies and no
current events**. Themes are defined by structural vocabulary: `sanctions`,
`force majeure`, `unplanned outage`, `storage target`, `chokepoint`. That
language will still describe a market-moving story in 2035. "Russia/Ukraine"
will not necessarily still be the story.

The only named entities are things that cannot move: straits, pipelines,
terminals, gas fields and hubs.

To add a theme, copy the shape of an existing one. To retire one, set
`"active": false`. The broad-search sources build their queries straight out of
this file, so a new theme starts being searched for on the next run with no
other change.

### `config/sources.json`

Where the news comes from, how hard to poll it and how much to trust it.
Every source has an `active` flag; turning one off is a one-word edit.

## Scoring, in short

Every item gets a 0-100 score built from: source authority, theme matches,
geographic proximity to the book, whether the event is unplanned, whether the
language is urgent, whether a real quantity is attached, and how recent it is.
Opinion pieces are penalised, but only when nothing concrete is present.

Every score ships with its own breakdown, visible in the widget by clicking a
row. Nothing is a black box.

Three behaviours are worth knowing because they are not obvious:

- **Planned versus unplanned is the most important distinction in the feed.**
  Routine maintenance is already in the curve. An unplanned outage is the news.
  Anything explicitly described as planned can never raise a critical alert,
  however high it scores.
- **Alert level moves monotonically with score.** A 79 will never be flagged
  more urgently than an 81. An earlier version promoted primary sources across
  the threshold and it made the ranking impossible to read at a glance.
- **Criticals are rate-limited** to the strongest four in any six-hour window.
  The rest stay in the feed and stay visible, they just stop flashing the
  drawer. An alert that fires constantly is an alert nobody looks at.

### How long items stay visible

Scores decay on a 9 hour half-life, configurable in `themes.json` under
`recency`. Roughly:

| Age  | Major unplanned outage | Geopolitical event | Routine regulation |
|------|------------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| 0h   | 83 critical            | 79 high            | 49 normal          |
| 6h   | 73 high                | 68 high            | 39 off display     |
| 12h  | 56 normal              | 51 normal          | 27 off display     |
| 18h  | 40 off display         | 36 off display     | 18 off display     |
| 24h  | 28 off display         | 25 off display     | 12 off display     |

A big story is critical for around four hours, high for nine, and off the
default view inside a day. Items are retained for 14 days and stay searchable,
they just stop competing for attention.

The first version had a decay floor, which meant a major story settled at
score 57 and sat there indefinitely. Tuesday's outage floated above Thursday's
news and nothing marked it as over.

### Developing stories

Decay alone does not solve it. A big story usually gets follow-ups, and the
original should be retired by its own update rather than slowly fading.

`src/supersede.py` handles this. Two items are the same thread when they share
an entity, share a theme and fall inside the supersession window. The newest
becomes the visible one, badged "updated"; the rest are demoted, flagged
`hidden` and linked to what replaced them. Nothing is deleted.

    Mon 16:40  Kollsnes outage to last into Wednesday      -> hidden
    Tue 09:12  Unplanned outage at Kollsnes, 18 mcm/day    -> hidden
    Tue 11:30  Kollsnes returns to full capacity           -> VISIBLE, "updated"

Sharing a theme is the guard that stops "Freeport train halted" being retired by
"Freeport announces expansion" three days later. Same asset, different kind of
event, no supersession.

Deduplication deliberately refuses to merge a resolution into the outage it
resolves. Supersession is what links them, so "it is fixed" can never be
hidden behind "it broke".

Run `tests/test_pipeline.py` after changing any of this. Every test in there
exists because that behaviour was wrong at some point during the build.

## Hosting

`.github/workflows/ingest.yml` runs the job every five minutes on GitHub
Actions and commits `data/news.json` back to the repo.

**Use a public repo.** GitHub Actions minutes are unlimited on public repos and
capped at 2,000 a month on private ones, which a five-minute schedule blows
through in about three days. The repo contains only public feed URLs and the
scoring config; every credential sits in Actions Secrets.

**Do not run this on Netlify scheduled functions.** Netlify's 2026 free plan is
credit-metered across bandwidth, builds and function compute, and when the
credits run out the free plan stops serving traffic. A job running every five
minutes against that shared pool is a live risk to pesomnia.com itself.

GitHub's scheduled workflows have a five-minute floor and can be delayed at
peak times. That is acceptable here: the feeds that need genuine speed are the
REMIT ones, and they publish on their own schedule regardless.

If sub-five-minute latency ever matters, Cloudflare Workers has a free tier
with cron triggers and would be the next step. It is not needed on day one.

## Copyright position

The feed stores and displays a **headline, a source name, a link and a short
snippet**. Never full article text. That is the safe side of the line for
republishing third party content on an internal screen, and it also keeps the
widget readable. Do not be tempted to pull full text into the snippet field.

Two sources carry specific caveats, both flagged in `config/sources.json` and
both switched **off** by default:

- **Google News RSS** works, but its terms say you may not scrape or display
  the content on another site without permission. It is also slow, with a
  median item age of around 6.6 days in a July 2026 sample. Treat it as a
  discovery tool for your own reading, not a feed to republish.
- **GDELT** is free and excellent for geopolitical reach, but some sources
  describe the free tier as non-commercial. Confirm the licence before
  switching it on for PES use.

Neither is needed for the system to work. The operational feeds and publisher
RSS carry the load.
